H	Abstract
P	Federated learning (FL) lets hospitals train a shared skin-lesion classifier without sharing images, but on real sites it is less accurate than centralized training, and the trained model can still reveal which images it was trained on. Studies usually report the accuracy loss as one average over sites and do not measure this leakage. We measure both on HAM10000, whose four image sources act as hospitals, and repeat the key experiments on the six centres of Fed-ISIC2019. Removing device differences between sites while keeping their images and disease mix recovers 35% to 49% of the accuracy gap of batch-norm (BN) models on HAM10000 and 28% to 42% on Fed-ISIC2019, and four controlled comparisons point to the averaged BN statistics as the reason. Mixing does not help layer-norm models, and ConvNeXt-atto gives the best federated model (0.741 balanced accuracy, against 0.661 for EfficientNet-B0), although on Fed-ISIC2019 its lead needs three times the benchmark's training steps and holds only in the per-centre metric. Under a strong membership attack (RMIA), federated models leak less than centralized ones, but a server that sees each site's BN model learns more than one that sees only their average. Differential privacy works only on the last layer; pretraining the frozen backbone on public dermoscopy images from another hospital raises its accuracy at epsilon = 3 from 0.43 to 0.55, still 0.19 below the best non-private model.
P	Index Terms: federated learning, differential privacy, membership inference, batch normalization, skin lesion classification, data heterogeneity.
H	I. Introduction
P	A skin-cancer classifier needs many images of every lesion type, taken with many devices. No single clinic has that much data, and patient images usually cannot leave the hospital [1]. Federated learning (FL) [2] lets hospitals train one model together while each keeps its images; only model updates go to a server.
P	This setup comes with two hopes, and neither holds by default. The shared model should come close to one trained on all the data in one place, yet accuracy drops when hospitals differ in patients, disease mix and imaging devices [3], [4]. Keeping images on site should protect patients, yet a trained model can reveal whether an image was in its training set [5]-[7]. Differential privacy (DP) [8] puts a formal limit on this leakage, but it costs accuracy, and the cost grows when each hospital has little data.
P	In the skin-lesion FL studies we reviewed, including the Fed-ISIC2019 benchmark of FLamby [9] and the study of Chen et al. [10], the accuracy loss is reported as one number averaged over sites, and privacy is argued from the design rather than measured. One number does not show which difference between hospitals causes the loss, and so which fix to try. Without an attack, we also do not know how much a federated model leaks or what formal protection costs on real hospitals.
P	We study both questions on HAM10000 [11], whose four image sources act as hospitals, and repeat the key experiments on the six centres of Fed-ISIC2019. Each main claim rests on a comparison in which only one thing changes: a device-mixing control keeps each hospital's size and disease mix but removes device differences; a pair of ResNet-50 models differs only in the normalization layer; and on Fed-ISIC2019, two architectures are compared at the benchmark budget and with three times as many training steps. We measure leakage with RMIA [12], a strong membership attack, also for a server that sees each hospital's model before averaging, and we apply DP at three depths of the network. In short, we measure what FL costs on real hospitals in accuracy and in privacy, trace part of the accuracy cost to device differences that act through batch-normalization statistics, and reduce part of the privacy cost with public features under DP.
P	This work makes four contributions.
P	Section II reviews related work, Section III describes the methods, Section IV reports the results, Section V discusses their limits, and Section VI concludes.
H	II. Related Work
H	A. Site differences and normalization
P	FedAvg [2] averages the weights that sites send back after local training, and it loses accuracy when sites hold different data [3]. Studies usually simulate different label mixes with Dirichlet splits [3]; a feature shift, where the same class looks different at each site, comes from different scanners [13]. FedProx [4] and server momentum (FedAvgM) [3] change the optimization, and we test both. FedBN [13] keeps the batch-normalization (BN) layers at each site, which requires knowing the site of each test image; we report it with and without this knowledge. Hsieh et al. [14] showed that averaging BN statistics across sites hurts when the sites hold different data, and that group normalization (GN) [15] avoids this. Transformers with layer normalization (LN) coped better with different clients [16], and ResNets without normalization beat BN ResNets in federated training, including on Fed-ISIC2019 [17]. These studies show that BN suffers when sites differ, but not which kind of difference causes the harm. We remove only device differences, keep each hospital's disease mix fixed, and measure how much of the loss goes away for BN, GN and LN models.
H	B. Skin-lesion data and benchmarks
P	HAM10000 [11] collects dermoscopy images from several sources and devices and is the training set of ISIC 2018 [18]. Lesions photographed more than once leak across splits made by image [19], so we split by lesion. Models learn signs of the imaging device instead of lesion features [20] and lose accuracy on new centres [21]; these results come from centralized training, and we measure what such differences cost in FL. Colour constancy methods such as Shades of Gray [22] reduce the colour tint of each device, and we test one as a fix in FL. FLamby [9] released Fed-ISIC2019, which splits ISIC 2019 into six centres by imaging system and trains for a fixed, short budget. Later runs with longer training [23] or with the checkpoint chosen on validation [24] report different FedAvg accuracy; we reproduce the original protocol and add longer budgets. Chen et al. [10] build clients from real skin-lesion sources and report accuracy; we report per-site accuracy and membership leakage of the same models.
H	C. Membership inference
P	Membership inference asks whether a record was in a model's training data [5]. The loss threshold attack is closely tied to overfitting [6], and Nasr et al. [7] studied membership inference in FL with access to model updates. Carlini et al. [25] argued for reporting the true-positive rate at a low false-positive rate and proposed LiRA, which needs many shadow models; RMIA [12] reaches similar power with a few reference models. We use offline RMIA with eight reference models and report this rate at a 1% false-positive rate together with the AUC. The risk can differ between groups even when the average looks low [26], so we also compute it per site and per class. Secure aggregation [27] hides the update of each site from the server; we measure what it hides by attacking each site model and their average.
H	D. Differential privacy
P	DP-SGD [8] clips the gradient of each example and adds Gaussian noise at every step. Tramèr and Boneh [28] showed that under DP, a linear classifier on good fixed features is a strong baseline, and that better features help more than a better private optimizer. We apply this idea to federated training on medical images, using features learned from public dermoscopy images of another hospital, and compare them with ImageNet features at the same budget. Ziller et al. [29] measured the cost of DP on medical images, including HAM10000, in centralized training; we measure it across four real sites, each spending its own budget, at three depths. The cost of DP falls most on small groups [30]; we test whether this holds for small hospitals.

A box with a specific label, such as the linear Classifier, is the same in every experiment. The numbered marks show where the study changes or measures something: the site split (①), the averaging at the server, which also averages the BN statistics (②), the site models that the server sees (③), and the attacks on the final model (④).

H	A. Setup and metrics
P	Each image has one of C = 7 labels. The training data are held by K = 4 sites, and site k holds n_k of the n images. In each round the server sends the global weights to every site, each site trains on its own images, and the server averages the results in proportion to site size:
P	Always predicting nevus gives about 67% accuracy, so the Accuracy box uses balanced accuracy, the mean recall over classes, which is also the ISIC 2018 metric [18]:
P	The cost of FL is the gap to a centralized model with the same backbone and recipe. To split this gap, each design is also trained on device-mixed sites (Section III-B). The device share is the accuracy that FedAvg gains when moving from the real to the device-mixed sites, divided by the gap. Both sets of sites hold the same images with the same class counts per site, so the device share is the part of the gap that goes away when only device differences are removed.
P	Training and validation images are split by lesion; a split by image would put a second photo of the same lesion into training for 38% of the validation images. This gives 8,583 training and 1,432 validation images. The official ISIC 2018 test set (1,512 images) is scored once per final model, and a check with perceptual hashes found no confirmed duplicates with the training data. The test set has no source label, so per-site results and model selection use the validation split. Repeated seeds (three, or five for the main settings) show whether a result is stable across runs, and a bootstrap over lesions shows how much a per-site or per-class result depends on the particular images. On Fed-ISIC2019 we report the benchmark metric, the mean of per-centre balanced accuracy on the test sets, and also balanced accuracy on all test images together (pooled).
H	B. Training images and site split
P	The Training images box takes HAM10000 or Fed-ISIC2019, and the Site split box (①) divides the images into sites, either by their real source or with the device-mixing control. HAM10000 has 10,015 images of 7,470 lesions in seven classes (Table I). Its four sources, three collections from the Department of Dermatology in Vienna and one practice in Queensland [11], serve as the sites (Table II). They differ nine-fold in size, and 94% of the labels at vidir_molemax come from follow-up rather than histopathology, as in a clinic that monitors moles. Fed-ISIC2019 [9] has six centres (BCN, the four HAM10000 sources and MSK) with 23,247 images in eight classes in its official split.

The images differ much more (Fig. 2). P	The device-mixing control builds a second set of sites from the same training images. Each mixed site keeps nearly the same number of images per class as the real site it replaces, but draws its lesions at random from all four sources and keeps all images of a lesion together. The two sets of sites therefore differ only in which device each site's images come from; size, disease mix and the images themselves stay the same. On Fed-ISIC2019 the centre labels of the training images are shuffled within each class, which keeps the class counts of every client.
H	C. Preprocessing and augmentation
P	The Preprocessing box has two variants on HAM10000. P1 resizes each image to 224 pixels on the short side and crops the centre. P2 adds Shades of Gray colour constancy [22] with p = 6, which uses only the pixels of each image and so needs nothing from other sites. The Augmentation box applies random flips and 90-degree rotations on HAM10000. On Fed-ISIC2019 both boxes follow the benchmark code.
H	D. Backbone and normalization
P	The Backbone box stands for six networks, each kept with the Norm layer it was pretrained with: two BN models (EfficientNet-B0 [31], ResNet-18 [32]), two LN models (ConvNeXt-atto [33], DeiT-tiny [34]), and a ResNet-50 pair with BN and with GN. The pair shares its architecture and pretraining recipe and differs only in the Norm layer, so a difference between them comes from normalization. To fit a 4 GB GPU, the pair runs at 160 pixels with batch size 32. All backbones start from ImageNet weights, except in the proposed design, which uses weights learned from public dermoscopy images (Section III-E). The Classifier box is always one linear layer.
H	E. Local update and aggregation
P	The Local update box has two forms. In full training, each site trains the whole network with class-weighted cross-entropy. Class weights are inverse class frequencies, computed either from the counts of all sites together (global), which requires each hospital to report its number of cases per class, or from each site's own counts (local). The Aggregation box (②) is FedAvg in the reference design, compared with FedProx [4] (mu = 0.01, 0.1 and 1), FedAvgM (beta = 0.9) and FedBN [13]. FedAvg averages all weights, including the running statistics of the BN layers. FedBN keeps the BN layers at each site; it is scored with each site's own model on that site's validation images and, on the test set, where the source is unknown, with an ensemble of the four site models.
P	In the DP form, each site trains with DP-SGD [8]: Poisson sampling, per-example clipping at norm 1, Gaussian noise and Rényi DP accounting at delta = 1e-5, with each site spending its own budget epsilon in {8, 3, 1} over all rounds. DP is applied to the classifier on frozen features (L0) or to the last one or two blocks plus the classifier (L1, L2). The frozen part stays in evaluation mode, so BN uses fixed statistics and the per-example gradients stay valid. A run with clipping only and a run without DP separate the cost of clipping from the cost of noise. The learning rate for each epsilon is chosen on validation from {16, 4, 1}.
P	The proposed design follows the finding of [28] that DP accuracy depends mostly on the quality of the frozen features. We pretrain the same backbone centrally on public dermoscopy images from another hospital, the BCN training images of ISIC 2019, freeze it, and let the four HAM10000 sites train only the classifier with DP-SGD (Fig. 3). It differs from the ImageNet version only in the frozen weights, so any change in accuracy at a given epsilon comes from the features. Before pretraining, BCN images are compared with all HAM10000 images by perceptual hash and fine-structure correlation, and confirmed duplicates are removed, so no HAM10000 image enters the public data.

H	F. Membership attacks
P	The Membership attack box (④) takes the view of an honest-but-curious server, which follows the protocol but tries to learn from what it receives, or of anyone who later gets the global model. The attacker tries to tell whether a given image was used in training. Members are training images and non-members are validation images; both come from the same distribution because the split is by lesion. The loss threshold attack [6] is a weak baseline, and offline RMIA [12] is the main attack. For each backbone, eight reference models are trained centrally on halves of the training set, split by lesion into four pairs of halves, so each training image is unseen by four reference models and each validation image by all eight. RMIA divides the target model's probability of the true label by a reference term computed from the models that did not see the image; the weight of this term is tuned with a reference model acting as the target, never with the real target. We report the AUC of the attack and the true-positive rate at a 1% false-positive rate [25].
P	Without secure aggregation [27], the server sees each site's model after local training (③). We simulate one extra round starting from the best global model of each FedAvg run. The cosine schedule makes the learning rate of that late round very small, so local training uses the learning rate of round 10, when updates have their usual size. We attack each site's own model, the averaged model and the global model before the round, with the site's training images as members and its validation images as non-members. The three models differ only in what the server sees, so the comparison measures what secure aggregation hides.
H	G. Hypotheses and decision rules
P	Each run was logged with its expected result before it started. A design replaces the reference only if it is better on validation in all three seeds and by more than 0.02 on average. A part of a final design is kept only if removing it lowers validation accuracy by more than 0.02 in all three seeds. The device effect counts as repeated on Fed-ISIC2019 if mixed minus source is above 0.03 in every seed. Differences between sites or classes are claimed only when bootstrap intervals do not overlap. Five hypotheses are tested: M1, M2, A, H1 and H2.
H	H. Training details
P	On HAM10000 all models are trained at 224 pixels with AdamW [35], learning rate 3e-4 and weight decay 1e-4, batch size 64, class-weighted cross-entropy and mixed precision. The centralized model trains for 15 epochs with a one-cycle schedule. Federated runs use 30 rounds of one local epoch with all four sites and a cosine learning-rate decay over rounds. The Final global model box is the round with the best validation accuracy, or the best epoch for centralized runs; on Fed-ISIC2019, which has no validation split, every run is scored at the end of training. DP runs at L0 train the classifier for 30 rounds with an expected batch of 256.
P	On Fed-ISIC2019 we first follow the FLamby protocol: EfficientNet-B0, weighted focal loss, centralized Adam at 5e-4 for 20 epochs, and federated SGD at 0.01 with 9 rounds of 100 local steps, scoring the final model. To compare architectures we then train EfficientNet-B0 and ConvNeXt-atto with the HAM10000 recipe under three federated budgets: 9 rounds of 100 steps; the same steps over 27 rounds, which changes only how often the server averages; and three times the steps (27 rounds of 100), matched by 60 centralized epochs.
P	FOOTNOTE:All experiments run on one laptop GPU with 4 GB of memory. A federated run on HAM10000 takes about 9 minutes with ConvNeXt-atto and 14 with EfficientNet-B0, a DP run at L0 a few minutes on stored features, and a long-budget run on Fed-ISIC2019 about 35 minutes. ResNet-50 at 224 pixels needed 6.8 GB, hence the 160-pixel pair. Runs resume from checkpoints and log their settings, code hash, data-split hash and learning curves.
H	IV. Results
P	Unless stated otherwise, numbers are validation balanced accuracy on HAM10000, averaged over three seeds. A difference is called consistent when it has the same sign in every seed, and within noise otherwise; claims about single sites or classes rest on bootstrap intervals.
H	A. Device share of the federation gap
P	The real and device-mixed sites hold the same images with the same class counts per site, so the change in accuracy between them comes from devices (Table III, Fig. 4). For the three BN models, FedAvg loses 0.10 to 0.14 against centralized training, and device mixing wins back about 0.05 of it, a device share of 35% to 49%. For the two LN models the gap is smaller (0.076 and 0.036) and mixing does not help: ConvNeXt-atto changes by -0.004, and DeiT-tiny drops by 0.044 and stays below the real sites in all three mixing draws. Devices thus explain a large part of the gap for BN models and no measurable part for LN models.

P	The smaller gap also gives a better federated model. With five seeds, FedAvg reaches 0.741 +/- 0.013 with ConvNeXt-atto and 0.661 +/- 0.010 with EfficientNet-B0 (mean +/- standard deviation), and the seed ranges do not overlap (0.719 to 0.752 against 0.646 to 0.672); on the test set the two score 0.662 and 0.597. Centralized, ConvNeXt-atto is ahead by only 0.035 (Table III), so most of the difference appears only in FL.
H	B. Mechanism of the device effect
P	Four results point to the BN statistics that FedAvg averages (M2, mark ② in Fig. 1), and none to the direction of local updates (M1). Mixing raised FedAvg accuracy by 0.047 for EfficientNet-B0 while the cosine similarity between site updates changed by at most 0.05, so M1 is rejected. For M2, first, mixing helps all three BN models and neither LN model. Second, in the ResNet-50 pair, mixing gains 0.051 with BN, consistent across seeds, and 0.014 with GN, within noise. Third, FedBN, which keeps the BN statistics at each site, gains 0.080 over FedAvg for EfficientNet-B0 and 0.040 for ResNet-18 on the real sites, but only 0.013 and 0.006 on the mixed sites. Fourth, colour constancy (P2) leaves centralized accuracy unchanged and raises FedAvg by 0.024 for EfficientNet-B0 but only 0.006 for ConvNeXt-atto.
P	Both direct fixes have practical limits. FedBN needs the site of each image: with an ensemble of the four site models, as on the test set, it scores 0.639 against 0.659 for FedAvg. GN avoids the device effect but is weaker overall: on the mixed sites, ResNet-50 with BN beats GN by 0.035 in every seed.
P	The rest of the gap, about 60% for BN models and nearly all of it for LN models, remains when only disease mix and site size differ, and no design we tried reduced it. FedProx with mu = 1 cut the drift of the largest site by a factor of three without improving accuracy (+0.009), and FedProx and FedAvgM brought no gain for ConvNeXt-atto (FedAvgM fell to 0.597). Per site, only rosendahl has a gap whose bootstrap interval excludes zero ([0.04, 0.21]); the point gap of vienna_dias (0.31) rests on 74 validation images and is not significant.
H	C. Ablation
P	Each final design is tested by changing one part at a time, with the rule of Section III-G (Table IV). Design 1 is ConvNeXt-atto with FedAvg, 30 rounds of one local epoch, global class weights and P1. ImageNet pretraining, the ConvNeXt-atto backbone and global class weights pass the rule, and server momentum (FedAvgM) is clearly worse than FedAvg; the other changes are within noise. Global class weights therefore stay, at the cost that each hospital reports its number of cases per class.
P	Design 2 is a frozen EfficientNet-B0 with DP-SGD on the classifier (L0), 30 rounds and P1; its learning rate is chosen on validation for each epsilon. At epsilon = 3, 30 rounds and the EfficientNet-B0 backbone pass the rule against 10 rounds and ConvNeXt-atto, while 60 rounds, P2 and a ViT-S backbone are within noise. The proposed design (Section IV-E) is Design 2 with the ImageNet weights of the frozen backbone replaced by weights pretrained on public BCN images.

P	Leakage differs more between classes than between sites. For the centralized EfficientNet-B0 under the loss attack, AKIEC reaches an AUC of 0.872 and nevi 0.536 (AKIEC is above 0.84 and nevi below 0.55 in every seed), while across sites the AUC ranges only from 0.525 at vidir_molemax to 0.700 at rosendahl, and the smallest site, vienna_dias (0.623), leaks less than rosendahl and vidir_modern. H2 therefore holds for classes and is rejected for sites. Across the seven classes, leakage goes with the train-validation loss gap (Spearman 0.75) more than with class size (-0.46): it reflects memorization rather than rarity, and VASC is rare but leaks little.
P	A curious server that sees each site's model before averaging learns more than one that sees only the average (mark ③ in Fig. 1). For EfficientNet-B0, the rosendahl model gives an RMIA AUC of 0.672 and a true-positive rate of 0.118, against 0.577 and 0.044 for the averaged model; for vidir_modern the AUC rises from 0.622 to 0.676. With the smaller learning rate of the best round the direction is the same and the differences are smaller (rosendahl 0.585 to 0.658). For ConvNeXt-atto the difference is small or absent. For EfficientNet-B0 the averaged model leaks about as much as the global model before the round, so with secure aggregation the server learns little more than anyone who holds the final model.
H	E. Cost of differential privacy
P	Clipping alone costs little; most of the cost of DP comes from the noise. With ImageNet features at L0, clipping lowers accuracy from 0.617 to 0.602, and the noise at epsilon = 3 lowers it further to 0.430 (Table VI). Training deeper layers helps without DP (the last two blocks of ConvNeXt-atto reach 0.702), but with DP at L1 and L2 accuracy falls to between 0.15 and 0.24, near chance (0.14), at every epsilon, also with a batch of 1024 and a learning rate of 3e-4. Formal privacy is thus possible only at L0. There, ConvNeXt-atto stays below EfficientNet-B0 at every epsilon (0.409, 0.353 and 0.276 at epsilon = 8, 3 and 1), so hypothesis A is rejected; among eight frozen ImageNet backbones, EfficientNet-B0 is the best at epsilon = 3.

The gain does not come from shared images: the perceptual hash flagged 7,463 candidate pairs between BCN and HAM10000, and none was a confirmed duplicate.
P	The cost of DP is uneven. At epsilon = 3, vienna_dias needs a noise multiplier of 8.2 to spend its budget and vidir_molemax only 2.5, yet vidir_molemax loses the most accuracy and vienna_dias the least, so the site part of H1 is rejected. Among classes, dermatofibroma loses the most, while melanoma, already low without DP, does not. Centralized DP at L0 is about 0.04 better than federated DP.
H	F. Second dataset and training budget
P	Our run of the FLamby protocol on Fed-ISIC2019 agrees with the original results within their spread. We obtain 0.731 centralized, 0.624 with FedAvg and 0.652 with FedProx, against 0.708 +/- 0.016, 0.539 +/- 0.097 and 0.544 +/- 0.074 (mean +/- standard deviation over five seeds) in the results released with FLamby [9]. The per-centre pattern also repeats: rosendahl is the weakest centre even when centralized (0.630; FLamby 0.623), and MSK drops most under FedAvg (0.415; FLamby 0.387). Under this budget, device mixing raises FedAvg by 0.030 (device share 28%), exactly at the threshold for a repeated effect.
P	The ranking of the two architectures depends on the budget (Table VII). With 9 rounds of 100 steps they tie, and spreading the same steps over 27 rounds changes nothing. With three times the steps, ConvNeXt-atto leads by 0.043 in the per-centre metric, consistent across seeds. On all test images together the two tie (0.700 against 0.699), because ConvNeXt-atto gains most at the smaller centres and is slightly behind at BCN, the largest one. EfficientNet-B0 stays near 0.64 under every budget while ConvNeXt-atto keeps improving, as on HAM10000 (Fig. 6), where the mean curve of EfficientNet-B0 levels off within ten rounds and ConvNeXt-atto improves until rounds 25 to 29.

H	V. Discussion and Limitations
P	On real hospitals, about 40% of the FL gap for BN models comes from imaging devices, and four comparisons point to the averaged BN statistics as the reason. An LN backbone removes that part without needing the source of each image, which FedBN needs, and on Fed-ISIC2019, with a long enough budget, mixing again helps the BN model more than the LN model. The rest of the gap remains when only disease mix and site size differ, and no design we tested reduced it.
P	The Fed-ISIC2019 results also hold a lesson on method. Under the FLamby budget the two architectures tie; with three times the steps they differ by 0.043, because EfficientNet-B0 levels off early while ConvNeXt-atto keeps learning. A short benchmark budget can therefore reverse a comparison between architectures in FL. Had we stopped at the first budget, we would have concluded that the LN advantage was specific to HAM10000. The comparison also depends on the metric: the 0.043 lead holds for the per-centre mean that FLamby reports, while on all test images together the two models tie, so comparisons on this benchmark should name the metric and report both.
P	On privacy, federated training lowers membership leakage but does not remove it, and a server that sees individual site models learns more than one that sees only their average, so secure aggregation is worth its cost, at least for BN models. Without DP, the choice of backbone barely changes leakage. At this scale, formal guarantees are possible only at the last layer, where public dermoscopy features raise accuracy by 0.12 to 0.13 at every epsilon. The design narrows the trade-off in our setting but does not remove it: at epsilon = 3 it stays 0.19 below Design 1, which itself offers only measured, not formal, protection.
P	These conclusions have limits. The federation is small, four sites and 8,583 training images, and the smallest site has 74 validation images, so most per-site differences are not significant. RMIA uses reference models trained centrally while the targets are trained with FL, so the measured leakage is a lower bound. The device share depends on how the mixed sites are built; the LN result holds on average, not at every site, and the drop of DeiT-tiny under mixing is unexplained. The rest of the gap mixes differences in disease mix and size with the cost of FL itself; a control with identically distributed sites would be needed to separate them. Beyond the ResNet-50 pair, LN and BN models also differ in architecture, so part of the LN advantage may come from the architecture itself. We did not study why EfficientNet-B0 is stronger centrally on Fed-ISIC2019, an advantage concentrated in one centre. DP is counted per image, with each site spending its own budget; DP at the level of whole sites is not practical with four sites. The public backbone was pretrained with one seed, BCN belongs to the same ISIC collection as HAM10000, and choosing the learning rate on validation makes validation scores slightly optimistic, so test scores are reported beside them. Source inference, gradient inversion and malicious sites are out of scope, and the 4 GB GPU limited the ResNet-50 pair to 160 pixels and ruled out larger backbones.
P	Future work should target the part of the gap caused by disease mix and site size, test longer budgets and larger backbones on more federations, and measure whether a federated model reveals a patient's hospital beyond what the image already shows.
H	VI. Conclusion
P	We measured what federated learning costs on real hospitals in accuracy and in privacy, on the four sources of HAM10000 and the six centres of Fed-ISIC2019. For BN models, imaging devices cause about 40% of the accuracy gap, and four comparisons point to the averaged BN statistics as the reason; an LN backbone avoids this part, while its advantage on Fed-ISIC2019 depends on the training budget and the metric. Federated training leaks less than centralized training, individual site models leak more than their average, and DP works only at the last layer, where public dermoscopy features recover part of its cost. At this scale no single design wins on both sides: Design 1 reaches 0.741 without formal guarantees, and the proposed DP design reaches 0.554 at epsilon = 3.
H	Reproducibility
P	All HAM10000 results use the lesion-level split with seeds 0 to 2 (0 to 4 for the main settings); Fed-ISIC2019 uses the official FLamby split. Every run records its settings, code hash, data-split hash, learning curves and the expected result written before it ran. Code, data splits and run logs are available from the author on request.
